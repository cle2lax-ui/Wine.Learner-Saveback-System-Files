## 6 — Mon 14 Sep · Quick Sips · Saint-Péray

**The idea.** Week one opened on Cornas: the only northern Rhône cru
that permits no white grape at all. Its immediate neighbour to the
south, the most southerly appellation in the region, makes **no red at
all**. Two adjoining crus, each of which has legislated the other's
entire production out of existence. That symmetry is the post.

### Page 1 — cover
**Title:** No Red at All
**Tagline:** Cornas's neighbour, in reverse

**Run-in 1** — `THE MIRROR.` Cornas permits one grape and it is Syrah;
no white wine may be made there. Cross the boundary south into
Saint-Péray, the most southerly appellation in the northern Rhône, and
the position inverts exactly. White only. Marsanne for the vast
majority of plantings, with some Roussanne.

**Run-in 2** — `THE REASON.` Slightly cooler than its immediate
neighbours, on limestone and granitic soils that hold water and drain
at the same time. Maximum yield 45 hL/ha against Cornas's 40. Higher
quality wines age on the lees for ten to twelve months, and some
producers stir them for body.

### Page 2 — detail
**Heading:** And It Makes Sparkling Wine

Traditional method sparkling, from the same two varieties — and,
per Ch. 7, **increasingly rare.** A traditional-method sparkling wine
inside a cru appellation better known for still white, quietly
disappearing. Typically good to very good, mid- to premium priced.

**Bottle slots — Steve's photography.** Slot A a still Saint-Péray,
Slot B the sparkling. **Sourcing risk, flagged now rather than on
render day:** Ch. 7 calls the sparkling increasingly rare and that is
likely to show up in availability. If Slot B cannot be filled, the
fallback is a single-bottle layout on the still wine with the
sparkling carried as text — *not* a substitution from another
appellation, which would break the whole premise.

Dashboard: 5-row white. No tannin row. No values pre-written.

*This entry describes the original static 1-page plan. What actually

**The idea.** One grape, one region, three appellations, three prices.
The ladder is built from the chapter's own numbers, and it is not a
quality ranking — it is a lesson in what yield, soil depth and slope
do to concentration.

| | **Crozes-Hermitage** | **Saint-Joseph** | **Hermitage** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size | ~1,700 ha, the largest | 50 km of appellation | **137 ha** |
| Max yield | 45 hL/ha | 40 hL/ha | 40 hL/ha |
| Ground | deeper, more fertile soils than Hermitage | wide range, hillside and not | thin, stony, south-facing |
| Harvest | machine possible on the flatter land | — | much work by hand, some terraced |
| Price | mid-priced, some premium | mid through super-premium | mostly super-premium |

**The line that carries the slide:** Crozes surrounds the hill of
Hermitage on three sides. The difference between the two is not the
grape and barely the climate — it is the depth of the soil and how
steeply it sits.

## 8 — Wed 16 Sep · GTR · Saint-Joseph

**Answer:** Saint-Joseph. Not named on page 1 or above the fold.

### Page 1 — clues
- **A.** It runs 50 kilometres, nearly the entire length of its region — from one famous white-wine appellation at the north end to the red-wine cru at the south.
- **B.** It takes its name from a single named site at its southern end, opposite the town across the river.
- **C.** It was extended in 1994, and the extension took in sites that are not on hillsides.
- **D.** The argument inside it now is whether to make it **smaller**.

### Page 2 — reveal
Nearly **90 per cent** red. Maximum yield 40 hL/ha. Marsanne and
Roussanne are permitted in small proportions in the red wine, though
in practice it is rare to include them. Quality and price run the full
range — good wines at mid-price through to very good and outstanding
at premium and super-premium. **Jean-Louis Chave** is based in the AOC
and has done much to raise its prestige, as has **Domaine Gonon**.

**Closing line:** Most appellations argue for more land. This one is
arguing itself back onto the hill.

**Why it is solvable:** clue A brackets it between Condrieu and Cornas,
both of which week one named. A reader who did week one has the
geography.

## 9 — Thu 17 Sep · FFFA · Hermitage

> **Overlap risk, flagged.** Hermitage already had a Field Guide slide
> in week one. This post must go past that slide, not restate it. The
> five below were chosen because none of them is basic appellation
> description.

1. **It is named after a man who quit.** Gaspard de Stérimberg, a twelfth-century crusader turned hermit. The hill has been producing wine since the Greco-Roman era; the name is comparatively recent.
2. **It is tiny, and a third of it is white.** 137 ha, virtually all planted — and one-third given to white wine, which is not what the appellation's reputation suggests.
3. **The best Syrah sites are the hottest ones.** The most famous climats sit at the **western end** of the hill, which records the highest temperatures — Le Méal among them.
4. **In very ripe years it makes a sweet wine.** A rare **Vin de Paille**, from grapes dried off the vine.
5. **Two philosophies own the hill.** Chapoutier pioneered an approach built on individual parcels; Chave is a long-standing supporter of blending across the whole appellation. The co-operative **Cave de Tain owns 15 per cent** of it.

**Cover:** the hill itself. `photos/nr_hermitage_hill.jpg` is already in
the repo from week one.

## 10 — Sat 19 Sep · Split Decision · Viognier in Côte-Rôtie

**The idea.** Week one's Split Decision took whole-bunch fermentation.
Week one's FFFA took Viognier. This post is where those two meet: the
one appellation in France where a white grape is permitted in a great
red wine, and the question of whether anyone should still use it.

**The rule.** Côte-Rôtie permits **up to 20 per cent Viognier** in a
wine otherwise made from Syrah. When both are used they **must be
co-fermented** — not blended afterwards. In practice the proportion is
**often zero, and normally no more than 8 per cent.**

**POLE — CO-FERMENT.** Where Viognier is used it adds floral and
fruity aromas, and Côte-Rôtie's reputation rests on pronounced aroma
above all. The practice is native to the appellation, from the era when
the two were interplanted. Co-fermentation is a different operation
from blending and produces something blending cannot.

**POLE — SYRAH ALONE.** Côte-Rôtie is already the softest and least
full-bodied of the top northern Rhône appellations, against Hermitage
and latterly Cornas. Adding a white grape softens it further. And the
practice was born in a cooler era, when Syrah here struggled for
aromatic ripeness; the fruit arrives fully ripe now on high sunlight
interception, rapid drainage and poor stony soils.

**THE TURN.** The legal ceiling is 20 per cent and actual use is
usually under 8. The argument was settled in practice long before it
was settled in principle — the interesting question is why the ceiling
was never lowered to match.

**Closing question:** If almost nobody uses the allowance, should it
still exist?
