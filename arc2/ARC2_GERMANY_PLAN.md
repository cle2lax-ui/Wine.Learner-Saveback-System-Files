## The spine

German wine law does not classify by place. It classifies by **must
weight** — the sugar in the grape at the moment it is picked. That
single decision, formalised in the 1830s and locked into the fifth
German wine law of 1971, explains almost everything a student finds
confusing about the country: why a Kabinett can be bone dry or
medium-sweet, why `Piesporter Goldtröpfchen` and `Piesporter
Michelsberg` look like siblings and are not, and why the VDP and the
2021 revision are both, at heart, attempts to smuggle place back into
a ripeness system.

The Mosel is where that argument costs the most money. It is the
region with the steepest slopes, the highest labour cost per hectare,
the lowest proportion of dry wine (26 per cent trocken in 2021 against
just under 50 per cent nationally), and the greatest concentration of
vineyards whose reputations are older than the law that governs them.

**Arc thesis, one line:** *Germany grades sugar; the Mosel is where
that grading is most expensive and least adequate.*

Every post below sits somewhere on that line. Nothing in this arc is
"a nice fact about Germany" for its own sake.

## Why this order

**Monday opens narrow.** Arc 1 opened on Cornas — one appellation, one
rule, no white grape. Arc 2 opens on one legal definition. `trocken`
permits up to 9 g/L residual sugar where the sugar does not exceed
total acidity by more than 2 g/L, which is exactly the condition a
high-acid Riesling satisfies. So the word on the label is a
measurement, not a sensory promise. That is a two-slide idea, it is
counterintuitive, and it is the doorway to the whole arc.

**Tuesday goes wide.** The pillar carries the map, the ladder, the
region, and the reason the two do not fit each other. Slide 12 leaves
Saturday's question open rather than answering it.

**Wednesday rewards Tuesday.** The Saar is named on slide 8 of the
Field Guide and nowhere earlier in the arc. A reader who swiped
through Tuesday can solve Wednesday; a reader who did not has four
honest clues and a reason to go back. This is the same callback shape
Arc 1 used with Cornas, and it worked.

**Thursday is the variety, not the region.** FFFA carries the sends
signal and travels furthest outside the core strata. Riesling is the
most exportable subject in the arc.

**Saturday argues.** `Piesporter Michelsberg` (Grosslage, 600–1,800 ha
class, largely inexpensive) against `Piesporter Goldtröpfchen`
(Einzellage, among the finest Mosel Rieslings), sharing a village name
and almost nothing else. The 2021 law replaces Grosslage with
"Region"; whether that fixes anything is a real argument with two real
sides.

**Week 2 widens off the Mosel without leaving the chapter.** Ahr and
Baden on Monday, a price ladder on Tuesday, Franken on Wednesday, the
law itself on Thursday, and the arc's own thesis put to the audience
on Saturday.

### Flagged for verification — do not render until sourced

Caught in the first pass over the chapter. These are things a German
arc naturally reaches for and Ch. 11 does not supply:

1. **Mosel hectarage.** Ch. 11 gives Germany ≈100,000 ha (2018,
   seventh largest in Europe) and gives Rheinhessen as "just under a
   quarter of German vineyards," but gives **no hectare figure for the
   Mosel**. Arc 1's cover subtitle used a hectarage; this one cannot.
   The Field Guide cover subtitle has been written around the gap
   using the two figures Ch. 11 *does* give for Mosel — 91 per cent
   white, 62 per cent Riesling.
2. **Export price currency.** Ch. 11 states average export price rose
   from below 200 to 300 per hL between the 2000s and the mid-2010s.
   The currency symbol does not survive extraction cleanly. Slide 2
   therefore states the **rise as a proportion (about half again)**,
   not as an absolute with a currency mark.
3. **The Sonnenuhr sundials.** Ch. 11 names `Wehlener Sonnenuhr` and
   `Ürziger Würzgarten` as vineyards but says nothing about the
   physical sundials on those slopes or who built them. Attractive
   decode-layer material, entirely unsourced here. Not used.
4. **Scharzhofberg labelling without a village prefix.** Ch. 11 gives
   the village-prefix rule and its exceptions (Schloss Johannisberg,
   Schloss Vollrads) and separately names Scharzhofberg as the Saar's
   most famous vineyard. It does **not** say Scharzhofberg is one of
   the exceptions. A GTR clue drafted on that assumption was cut.
5. **"Mosel-Saar-Ruwer" as the former name of the Anbaugebiet.** Not
   in Ch. 11. Not used.

## Build dependencies

**Map (Field Guide slide 3) — open build question.** Germany has no
public equivalent of the INAO parcellaire that carried Arc 1. Ch. 11
gives 2,658 registered Einzellagen but no geometry. Two honest
options, same choice Arc 1 faced:

- **(a)** Real traced river courses (Mosel, Saar, Ruwer) from public
  hydrography + a real traced Rheinland-Pfalz / Anbaugebiet boundary,
  with the named villages as verified labelled points. Achievable,
  honest, and consistent with the locked standard in
  `pipeline-and-tools` — real boundary data, never freehand.
- **(b)** Source true Einzellage polygons if a public dataset exists.
  Not located as of this session.

Default to (a) unless (b) turns up. **Under no circumstances are
vineyard polygons drawn by hand.**
