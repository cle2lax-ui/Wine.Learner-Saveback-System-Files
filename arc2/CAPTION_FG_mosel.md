## CAPTION — paste as-is

German wine law does not classify by place. It classifies by how much sugar was in the grape when it was picked.

That principle dates to the 1830s and was locked into the fifth German wine law in 1971, and it explains almost everything students find confusing about the country — why a Kabinett can be bone dry or medium-sweet, why Piesporter Goldtröpfchen and Piesporter Michelsberg share a village name and nothing else, and why every reform since has been an attempt to smuggle place back into a ripeness system.

The Mosel is where that costs the most. Ninety-one per cent white, sixty-two per cent Riesling, gradients reaching 70 per cent, soil winched back up the slope as routine maintenance, and spraying that is often only practicable by helicopter. On those sites, only Riesling reliably commands a price that makes the farming sustainable — which is why, where consolidation was never practicable, you can still see vineyards that were simply given up on.

Twelve slides: the ladder from Kabinett to TBA and why not one rung means "sweet", what trocken actually measures, the Einzellage and the Grosslage, the Saar and the Ruwer, and the one Prädikat defined by temperature rather than sugar.

The best producers in the country already label their dry wines Qualitätswein trocken and reserve the Prädikat terms for wines with sugar. When the people best placed to climb the ladder decline to, the ladder is the thing worth examining.
