## CAPTION — paste as-is

Since 1971, the loudest thing on a German label was how ripe the grapes were. From the 2026 vintage, it's where they grew.

Germany's law now asks three separate questions of every bottle. Where did the grapes grow: Anbaugebiet, then Region, Ortswein and Lagenwein, with Erstes and Grosses Gewächs at the top. How ripe were they: Kabinett to Trockenbeerenauslese. And how dry is the finished wine. "Riesling Spätlese Trocken" answers all three, and the Prädikat ladder itself has not changed.

A Spätlese tells you how ripe the grapes were. It does not tell you whether the wine is sweet.

The Mosel is the region a ripeness-first law fit worst: ninety-one per cent white, sixty-two per cent Riesling, vines on slopes of up to 70 per cent. In 2021 only twenty-six per cent of its wine was labelled trocken, against sixty-four in Baden.

If you read our Northern Rhône guide, this is the same shift: the label becoming the place. But the top of Germany's new pyramid is only half built. In June 2026 Grosses Gewächs gained national legal standing, and a new committee is still deciding which vineyards qualify.

So: is Grosses Gewächs Germany's Grand Cru, or does the comparison fail because Burgundy classifies vineyards while GG designates wines? Tell us which you would defend.
